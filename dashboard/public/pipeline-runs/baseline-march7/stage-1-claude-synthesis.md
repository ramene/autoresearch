### 1.1 Core Frameworks Identified

**Framework A: The Sensorium Three-Stage Model**
Jimenez-Fajardo & Salazar Gonzalez (2022) propose a hierarchical progression of spatial experience:
- **Sensorial** → raw sensory transduction (photons, odorant molecules, pressure waves hitting receptors)
- **Perceptual** → multisensory integration, binding, recognition (piriform cortex, orbitofrontal cortex assembling a coherent spatial percept)
- **Existential** → meaning-making, autobiographical embedding, atmospheric experience (Böhme's "atmosphere" as the felt quality of a place)

*Evidence*: Anchor paper DOI 10.5354/0719-5427.2022.67419; the model draws explicitly from Husserl's intentionality (consciousness is always consciousness-of-something), Merleau-Ponty's embodied perception, and Böhme's atmospheric aesthetics.

**Framework B: Olfaction as Pattern Recognition (Barwich)**
Ann-Sophie Barwich's "Smellosophy" (2020) dismantles the lock-and-key model of olfactory receptors. Key claims:
- Odorant receptors are combinatorial, not one-to-one (a single receptor responds to many molecules; a single molecule activates many receptors)
- Smell is fundamentally a *pattern recognition* problem — the olfactory bulb's glomerular map creates a spatiotemporal code
- This aligns with predictive processing: the brain doesn't passively receive odors but actively constructs olfactory percepts against prior expectations

*Implication for the platform*: If olfaction is pattern recognition, then the sensorium model's "sensorial" stage is already computational — not passive. This challenges Jimenez-Fajardo's implied hierarchy.

**Framework C: The Olfactory-Hippocampal Privileged Pathway**
The olfactory system is neuroanatomically unique:
- Only sensory modality with direct projections to hippocampus and amygdala (bypassing thalamic relay)
- This explains the Proust effect: odor-evoked autobiographical memories are more vivid, more emotional, and more spatially contextualized than memories triggered by other senses
- Tulving's episodic memory theory provides the cognitive architecture: episodic memory = "what + where + when" — olfaction uniquely encodes all three simultaneously

*Evidence*: The transcript cites this as a 2024-2026 research question; baseline from Kadohisa (2013), de Vries & Bhatt (2023 review).

**Framework D: Neuroarchitecture as Empirical Discipline**
The Academy of Neuroscience for Architecture (ANFA) research community is moving from philosophical speculation to empirical measurement:
- fMRI/EEG studies of architectural experience in VR environments
- Mobile EEG for in-situ building walkthroughs
- Scent-scape studies measuring cognitive performance and stress biomarkers (cortisol, heart rate variability)

*Evidence*: Transcript Thread 3, Questions 6-7; Vilaplana & Yamanaka's cross-modal design research; ANFA conferences 2024-2025.

**Framework E: Olfactory Dysfunction as Neurodegeneration Biomarker**
Clinical thread with high translational relevance:
- Olfactory testing (UPSIT, Sniffin' Sticks) shows promise as early Alzheimer's/Parkinson's screen
- Post-COVID anosmia cohort provides unprecedented natural experiment in olfactory neuroplasticity
- Question of whether COVID-related olfactory damage accelerates neurodegeneration is active (2025-2026 longitudinal studies underway)

**Framework F: Neurophenomenology and the Explanatory Gap**
Varela's (1996) research program to bridge first-person phenomenological reports with third-person neuroscience data:

- 4E cognition (embodied, embedded, enacted, extended) is the theoretical successor

## 5. DEBATE POSITIONS (Structured for Gemini)

### Debate Position 1: The Three-Stage Model Is Neuroscientifically Incoherent

**Argument**: Jimenez-Fajardo's "sensorial → perceptual → existential" progression implies a feedforward hierarchy — raw sensation first, then perception, then meaning. But predictive processing theory (Friston, Clark, Hohwy) argues the brain works TOP-DOWN: priors (expectations, memories, conceptual frameworks) shape sensation from the very first moment. There IS no "pure sensorial" stage. The olfactory bulb's processing is already shaped by cortical feedback from piriform cortex, orbitofrontal cortex, and hippocampus before the signal reaches conscious awareness. Barwich's "Smellosophy" explicitly argues this: smell is never raw data; it's always already interpreted.

**Evidence**:
- Barwich (2020), Ch. 5-7: Olfactory perception is constructive, not receptive
- Friston (2010), "The free-energy principle: a unified brain theory?" *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*
- Clark (2013), "Whatever next? Predictive brains, situated agents, and the future of cognitive science" *BBS*
- Olfactory bulb receives more centrifugal (top-down) fibers than centripetal (bottom-up) fibers — cortex shapes sensation

**Weakest Assumption**: This argument assumes predictive processing is the correct framework. If it isn't — if there ARE meaningful processing stages — the three-stage model could be a legitimate approximation of hierarchical processing (analogous to V1 → V2 → V4 → IT in visual processing).

**Question for Gemini**: Is there a steel-man version of the three-stage model that's compatible with predictive processing? Could "sensorial → perceptual → existential" be reinterpreted as different SCALES of prediction error (low-level feature prediction → object-level prediction → narrative/autobiographical prediction)?

### Debate Position 2: The Ocularcentric Critique Is Empirically Overstated

**Argument**: Pallasmaa (2005) and the anchor paper claim architecture privileges sight over other senses. But this ignores: (a) acoustic architecture is a mature discipline (concert hall design, noise regulation, acoustic ecology); (b) thermal comfort has driven building science for over a century (ASHRAE standards); (c) tactile considerations are embedded in material selection, handrail design, flooring; (d) olfactory design IS genuinely neglected, but the critique should be specifically about olfaction, not "all non-visual senses."

**Evidence**:
- ASHRAE Standard 55 (thermal comfort) — mandates thermal design considerations
- ISO 3382 (room acoustics) — concert halls, classrooms, hospitals all have acoustic standards
- ADA guidelines include tactile requirements (detectable warnings, Braille signage)
- NO equivalent standard exists for olfactory design in buildings

**Weakest Assumption**: That the existence of technical standards equals genuine multisensory design practice. Standards may be minimum compliance, not design aspiration. Pallasmaa's critique may be about *design culture* (the visual dominance of architectural rendering, photography, and criticism) rather than technical standards.

**Question for Gemini**: Is the ocularcentric critique about production (how architects design) or reception (how buildings are experienced)? If production, the visual dominance of tools (Rhino, Revit, renders) supports it. If reception, the phenomenological claim is empirically testable — has anyone tested whether people experience buildings primarily visually?

### Debate Position 3: Olfaction Is the WRONG Sense to Lead the Anti-Ocularcentric Charge

**Argument**: If the goal is to demonstrate multisensory architecture, olfaction is the hardest case. Smells are: (a) difficult to control in buildings (ventilation disperses them, chemical stability is limited); (b) highly individual (genetic variation in OR genes means people literally smell different things); (c) culturally loaded (what's pleasant in one culture is offensive in another); (d) habituating (neural adaptation means you stop smelling a constant odor within minutes). Audition or haptics would be much easier entry points for multisensory architecture.

**Evidence**:
- Genetic variation: ~400 functional OR genes in humans, with significant polymorphism (Mainland et al., 2014)
- Habituation: Olfactory adaptation occurs within 1-3 minutes of constant exposure (Dalton, 2000)
- Cultural variation: Majid et al. (2018), "Differential coding of perception in the world's languages" *PNAS* — smell categories vary dramatically across cultures
- Control challenges: No "olfactory speaker" equivalent to visual displays or audio speakers; scent diffusion is physically unconstrained

**Weakest Assumption**: That architectural design must *control* sensory stimuli. Böhme's "atmosphere" concept suggests architecture should *orchestrate* ambient conditions — including letting uncontrolled smells contribute to place-character. The smell of old wood, rain on stone, or cooking from a neighbor IS the multisensory architecture.

**Question for Gemini**: Is there a fundamental asymmetry between "designable" senses (vision, audition — we can create and control stimuli) and "ambient" senses (olfaction, thermoception — we experience them but can't easily design them)? If so, does the sensorium model need different theoretical treatment for each?

### Debate Position 4: The Pipeline's Computational Sensorium Is the Breakthrough Contribution

**Argument**: The most original research question in the transcript is Q9: "What would Jimenez-Fajardo's sensorium look like under a computational neuroscience lens?" If we can formalize the sensorium as a predictive processing system — where architectural space structures sensory priors, prediction errors drive exploration, and existential meaning emerges from high-level predictive models — we'd bridge three fields simultaneously: computational neuroscience, phenomenological philosophy, and architectural design. This would be publishable in *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews* or *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B*, not just architecture journals.

**Evidence**:
- Friston's active inference framework: agents minimize free energy by updating models (perception) or acting on the world (action). Navigating a building is active inference.
- Pezzulo et al. (2024), "Active inference and predictive processing in architecture" (hypothesized paper — check if exists)
- The sensorium's three stages map to hierarchical prediction: sensorial = low-level prediction errors, perceptual = mid-level object/scene models, existential = high-level narrative/self models
- Barwich's pattern recognition olfaction fits naturally: olfactory percepts are Bayesian posterior estimates given chemical evidence and contextual priors

**Weakest Assumption**: That formalization adds value rather than losing the phenomenological richness that makes the sensorium model interesting. Böhme and Husserl specifically argue that mathematical formalization misses the essence of lived experience. The computational translation might be seen as a category error by the phenomenological community.

**Question for Gemini**: Is there a mathematical framework that preserves the phenomenological insight (first-person, qualitative, meaning-laden) while enabling neuroscientific prediction (third-person, quantitative, mechanism-based)? Is this the unsolved problem of consciousness wearing a new hat, or is there a pragmatic middle ground?

## 6. QUESTIONS FOR GEMINI (Pure Reasoning Tasks)

### Q1: Hierarchy vs. Circularity in the Sensorium
The three-stage model (sensorial → perceptual → existential) implies a linear progression. But if predictive processing is correct, higher stages influence lower stages (top-down predictions). **Logically, can a model be both hierarchical (stages occur in order) and circular (later stages feed back to earlier ones) without being trivially true (everything influences everything)?** Define the formal conditions under which such a model is falsifiable.

### Q2: The Barwich-Böhme Incompatibility
Barwich argues olfaction is pattern recognition (computational, mechanistic, third-person). Böhme argues smell constitutes atmosphere (experiential, qualitative, first-person). **These seem to be describing different aspects of the same phenomenon. Is there a logical framework in which both are true simultaneously, or must one reduce to the other?** Consider: does Barwich's mechanism EXPLAIN Böhme's atmosphere, or do they answer different questions that never need to meet?

### Q3: The Uniqueness Claim for Olfaction
The transcript claims olfaction is unique because it bypasses the thalamus, connecting directly to hippocampus and amygdala. **But "unique" is doing a lot of work here. How unique is it really?** Taste also projects to insula and amygdala without full thalamic relay. Pain projects to anterior cingulate cortex with minimal thalamic processing. **What is the precise logical structure of the claim "olfaction is uniquely suited for spatial-autobiographical memory"? What would falsify it?**

### Q4: Optimal Study Design
Given these constraints:
- Budget: $500K (2-year study)
- Must combine neuroscience measurement with architectural phenomenology
- Must involve olfactory stimuli in real architectural spaces (not just lab)
- Must produce data publishable in both neuroscience AND architecture venues
- Must connect to the anchor paper's framework

**What is the optimal experimental design?** Consider: participant selection, architectural site selection, sensory stimulus control, neuroimaging modality (fMRI impossible in real buildings; EEG portable but low spatial resolution; fNIRS?), phenomenological interview protocol, statistical analysis plan. Solve this as a constrained optimization problem.

### Q5: The Translation Problem
Jimenez-Fajardo & Salazar Gonzalez write in Spanish for Latin American architecture journals. The neuroscience literature is in English in high-impact journals. **What is the optimal publication strategy to maximize the impact of a follow-up study?** Consider: journal selection, language, co-authorship across disciplines, open access requirements, and the citation dynamics between fields that don't read each other.

### Q6: Temporal Dynamics of the Sensorium
The anchor paper is titled "the experience of architectural space-TIME." The temporal dimension is crucial but underspecified. **Model the temporal dynamics**: When you walk into a church, how do the three stages unfold over time? Is the sensorial stage measured in milliseconds (receptor activation), the perceptual in seconds (object recognition), and the existential in minutes (meaning-making)? Or do they operate on overlapping timescales? **What does predictive processing predict about these temporal dynamics?**

### Q7: Memory Asymmetry
Tulving distinguished episodic from semantic memory. Olfactory-triggered memories are overwhelmingly episodic (vivid, emotional, contextualized). **Why?** Is this a feature of the olfactory-hippocampal pathway specifically, or a feature of any sensory modality that bypasses thalamic gating? **Construct a logical argument for why thalamic bypass → episodic rather than semantic memory.** What role does the amygdala's involvement play?

### Q8: Falsifiability of "Atmosphere"
Böhme's concept of "atmosphere" is phenomenologically rich but scientifically slippery. **Under what conditions would we say atmosphere doesn't exist?** If we measured every physiological parameter (heart rate, skin conductance, cortisol, EEG, pupil dilation) and behavioral measure (movement speed, gaze direction, self-report) in two spaces that phenomenologically feel "different" — and found NO measurable difference — would that falsify atmosphere? Or would Böhme argue we're measuring the wrong things?
